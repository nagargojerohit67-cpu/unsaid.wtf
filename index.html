🌐 INTERNET CULTURE

AI NOW WRITES BETTER THAN MOST HUMANS.
STUDENTS ARE RELIEVED. ENGLISH TEACHERS ARE NOT.

Humanity spent thousands of years developing language, poetry, and literature — the Mahabharata, Shakespeare, Premchand — only to arrive at 2026 and ask a chatbot to write: "Hey guys, just wanted to share some thoughts…"

Artificial intelligence tools are now writing essays, captions, breakup texts, cover letters, wedding speeches, and LinkedIn posts that sound more emotionally mature than the people using them. Students have greeted this development with relief. English teachers have greeted it with the expression of someone who just watched their entire career's purpose dissolve into a text box.

Nowhere is this more visible than on Indian LinkedIn, which has become a performance art installation nobody auditioned for. The format is now standardized: "At the age of 22, I learned that failure is just success wearing chappals." This sentence was written by an AI. It was posted by a human. It received 4,700 reactions. Three people cried. None of them knew why.

"We can no longer tell whether the candidate is brilliant or simply subscribed to premium AI," said an unnamed recruiter, who has since begun using AI to read AI-written applications, creating a loop that no longer requires humans at any stage. The recruiter called this "efficiency." Philosophers call it something else.

🪳 INTERNET CULTURE SPECIAL — BREAKING: DEMOCRACY EVOLVING

COCKROACH JANTA PARTY
GOES VIRAL

India's unemployed youth were called cockroaches. They responded by forming a political party. Naturally.

A new satirical online movement called Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) has exploded across Indian social media — because when life hands you an insult from a constitutional bench, you do the only logical thing: register a domain and launch a manifesto.

The movement emerged after controversial remarks attributed to Justice Surya Kant, which many users interpreted as comparing unemployed youth and online activists to "cockroaches." The remarks triggered outrage. But instead of simply posting angry tweets — which, let's be honest, nobody reads after 48 hours — the internet did what it does best: turned the insult into a brand.

Within days, the account reportedly attracted tens of thousands of followers, launched a mock manifesto, and adopted the tagline: "Voice of the Lazy & Unemployed." This is, objectively, the most accurate political platform India has seen in years. No vague promises. Pure transparency. Five stars.

Politicians such as Mahua Moitra and Kirti Azad publicly interacted with the movement online, helping it spread further — proving once again that Indian politicians will engage with literally anything that has more than 10,000 followers.
